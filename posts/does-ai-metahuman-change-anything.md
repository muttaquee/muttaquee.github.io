Over the last few posts I have shown you what an AI MetaHuman can do. It can carry a brand instead of a logo. It can think and respond in real time instead of running a script. It can guide you through a property. It can open a car door when you ask it to, rather than just talking about the car.

Every time I show one of these, people say the same thing. Wow.

I have started to distrust that word.

Not because the reaction is fake, but because it is the wrong measure. A demo that makes people say wow has cleared a very low bar. It has proven that something looks impressive for thirty seconds. It has not proven that the thing works. And those are two completely different claims.

This post is about the gap between them, because closing that gap is what my PhD is actually for.

## The question underneath the demos

Here is the question I keep coming back to. When you put an embodied AI human into a virtual experience, does it change what people do, or does it just change how the experience feels in the moment?

Are people more engaged? Do they explore more, understand more, remember more? Are they more confident in the decision they walk away with? Are they more likely to choose the brand than they would have been on their own? Or is the MetaHuman a beautiful distraction that adds warmth to the room and nothing to the outcome?

I genuinely do not know the answer yet. That is the point. Nobody in this space should be claiming they know until they have tested it properly, and a lot of the excitement around virtual humans quietly skips that step.

## Why the field keeps skipping this

The technology is moving fast, and most of the energy goes into realism. Better faces, better voices, better lip sync, better motion. The implicit assumption is that if we make the virtual human convincing enough, the value follows automatically.

I do not think that assumption is safe. Making something feel human and making it useful are separate problems. A more lifelike agent could just as easily distract from the product, slow people down, or make them trust a confident voice that happens to be wrong. Realism is not the same as effectiveness, and sometimes it can work against it.

So the interesting research question is not how real can we make it. It is does the realness earn its place.

## How you actually test it

You cannot answer this by showing more people the demo and watching them smile. You need a comparison.

The core of my study is simple to describe. Take the same task, for example configuring and choosing a car in VR, and run it two ways. In one version, the person explores on their own, with the kind of solo configurator that brands already build. In the other, the same experience, but with an embodied AI agent, Siara, who greets them, notices hesitation, points things out, and guides them the way a good salesperson would.

Then you hold everything else constant and look at what changes.

The important part is what you choose to measure. Enjoyment is easy to capture and almost useless on its own, because people enjoy novelty. So the measures that matter are the ones tied to behaviour and outcome. How present and engaged did people feel. How long did they spend, and where. How confident were they in the decision they made. How likely were they to choose the brand. And, the measure I care about most, does any effect survive once the novelty has worn off, or does it fade the second time someone puts the headset on.

That last point is where a lot of shiny results quietly fall apart, so I want to design for it from the start rather than discover it later.

## The result I am prepared to find

Here is the part that I think matters most, and it is the part that is easy to leave out of a confident LinkedIn post.

The agent might not make a difference. The numbers might come back flat. People might engage just as much, decide just as confidently, and choose just as often without the MetaHuman as with it.

If that happens, it is not a failure. It is a finding, and an important one, because it would tell the whole industry that pouring effort into embodied guides is not automatically worth it. A result that says this does not work under these conditions is far more useful than a demo that only ever gets shown when it goes well.

I am building the study so that it can tell me I am wrong. If it could only ever confirm what I hope, it would not be worth running.

## Why this is the real work

The demos were never the destination. They were a way to build the thing I actually need to study. Siara opening a car door is not the achievement. The achievement will be knowing, with evidence, whether her being there changed anything for the person inside the headset.

That is the difference between building something impressive and doing research, and it is the part of this work that keeps me up at night in the good way.
